01 · PEOPLE & PARTICIPATION

The People Behind the Hive

The story begins with the people who keep Australia’s bees. Recreational beekeepers make up the overwhelming majority of participants, but participation is not evenly distributed. New South Wales and Victoria contain the largest registered beekeeper communities. This establishes who is involved before exploring where Australia's hives are concentrated.

The beekeeper composition was analysed to compare commercial and recreational participation. Only about 6% of registered beekeepers are commercial operators, while approximately 94% are recreational. This shows that Australian beekeeping has a very broad recreational participation base.

Source: ABARES ↗

Registered beekeeper numbers were compared across Australian states. New South Wales records 9,347 beekeepers and Victoria records 9,236, making them the two largest beekeeper communities shown. Tasmania, by comparison, records only 276 beekeepers.

02 · GEOGRAPHY & SCALE

A Country of Hives

The story now moves from people to place. Comparing hive density, total hive numbers and state profiles shows that the geography of Australian beekeeping changes depending on how scale is measured. Tasmania stands out for hive density and operating intensity, while New South Wales dominates several absolute measures.

Hive numbers were normalised by state land area to avoid larger states appearing more important simply because of their size. Tasmania records the highest hive density at approximately 347 hives per 1,000 km², followed by Victoria and New South Wales. This provides a more meaningful geographic comparison than using absolute hive totals alone.

Source: ABARES ↗

Total hive numbers and beekeeper intensity were analysed together. New South Wales has the largest hive population with 297,362 hives, but Tasmania records approximately 86.1 hives per beekeeper. Therefore, the state with the largest number of hives is not necessarily the state with the most intensive beekeeping activity.

Source: ABARES ↗

State beekeeping profiles were compared across beekeeper numbers, hive numbers, apiary access and hives per beekeeper. New South Wales performs strongly across several absolute measures, while Tasmania stands out for hives per beekeeper. This shows that no single state dominates every aspect of the industry.

Source: ABARES ↗

03 · LANDSCAPE DEPENDENCE

Honey and the Landscape

Having established where beekeeping activity occurs, the story turns to the landscapes that support it. Public land forms an important part of beekeeping operations in several states, while the distribution of public apiary sites in New South Wales shows how access to suitable locations extends across a broad area.

Public-land dependence was compared between the available 2014–15 and 2019 observations. Queensland shows the largest increase, rising from approximately 28% to 80%, while Tasmania increases from about 82% to 90%. The comparison indicates increasing dependence on public land in several states.

Source: ABARES ↗

The state profiles were normalised to compare different measures on a common scale. The states do not follow one consistent pattern: states with large beekeeper or hive populations are not necessarily the strongest in apiary access or hives per beekeeper. This highlights the different operating structures across Australia.

Source: ABARES ↗

Public apiary locations were mapped to examine their geographic distribution across New South Wales. The sites extend across a broad area rather than forming one isolated cluster, showing that public-land access for beekeeping is geographically dispersed throughout the state.

04 · TRADE OVER TIME

Australia in the Honey Trade

The final part of the story follows Australian honey from production into the market. Australia began the period with strong export surpluses, but growing imports progressively narrowed this advantage. Together, the following visualisations reveal the transition toward a more competitive and import-pressured trade environment.

Import and export values between 1996 and 2020 were analysed to examine Australia's changing trade position. Exports were much larger than imports during most earlier years, but imports rose strongly during the 2010s and exceeded exports in several later years.

Source: ABARES ↗

The trade balance was analysed as exports minus imports. Australia recorded large positive balances throughout many earlier years, but the balance became negative in 2014 and reached its largest deficit in 2016 at approximately AUD 34.9 million. A small positive balance returned in 2020.

Source: ABARES ↗

Imports, exports and trade balance were compared across individual years to identify the changing trade regime. Later years combine much stronger import values with weaker or negative trade balances, making Australia's shift from a strong export surplus toward increasing import pressure clearly visible.

Source: ABARES ↗

RETURN TO THE HONEY JAR

One industry, shaped by people, place, landscape and trade.

Behind every honey jar is a much larger system. Australia’s honey industry begins with a predominantly recreational beekeeping community, but hive scale and intensity vary considerably between states. Access to suitable landscapes and public apiary locations supports these operations, while growing imports have changed Australia's position in the international market. Together, the visualisations show that Australian honey is shaped not by one factor, but by the interaction between people, geography, landscape access and trade.
